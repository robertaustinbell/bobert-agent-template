# Anderson, “More Is Different” (1972)

## Provenance and coverage

A five-page PDF was inspected completely. PDF p. 1 is a text-based JSTOR cover; article pages 2–5 required OCR and were also visually inspected from high-resolution renders. Anderson's article occupies printed pp. 393–396 and ends with its references in the upper portion of PDF p. 5; the unrelated article “Natural Areas” begins below that boundary. The source synthesis excludes the subsequent article.

## Source claims

Anderson distinguishes reductionism—the claim that phenomena obey underlying laws—from a stronger constructionist claim that those laws alone provide a practical or conceptually sufficient route to reconstruct behavior at another scale. He argues that the second does not follow automatically from the first (PDF p. 2; printed p. 393).

In many-body physics, large-scale behavior can require effective concepts and collective variables not supplied merely by listing lower-level laws. He illustrates this through scale, symmetry, and collective phenomena such as crystals and superconductivity (PDF pp. 2–4; printed pp. 393–395).

Anderson explicitly cautions that there may be no useful parallel between complexity in many-body physics and complexity in biological and cultural systems beyond a general relationship between parts and whole (PDF p. 5; printed p. 396).

## Agent-design synthesis

**Operational synthesis, not a procedure stated verbatim by Anderson:** a component-level model can be correct yet use the wrong predictive representation for the target decision. Declare the level of description, identify the effective variables and interactions needed at that level, and test whether changing the representation changes the forecast or move.

Difficulty constructing higher-level behavior from lower-level rules is not proof of ontological irreducibility. Conversely, knowledge of lower-level rules does not by itself establish a useful higher-level prediction.

## Limits and rejected transfers

This template does not adopt:

- “emergence,” “more is different,” hierarchy, or scale as a mechanism without named interactions and effective variables;
- broken-symmetry or phase-transition mathematics for organizations, minds, markets, households, or agents without domain-specific evidence;
- the practical difficulty of construction as proof that higher-level behavior is irreducible in principle;
- lower-level correctness as sufficient evidence for higher-level predictive adequacy;
- a hierarchy of sciences as a hierarchy of worth, authority, purpose, or legitimacy;
- Anderson's physical examples as empirical validation of broad biological, cultural, organizational, or agent analogies.

## Disposition
