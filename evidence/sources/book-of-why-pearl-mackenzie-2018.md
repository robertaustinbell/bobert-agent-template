## Source claims

Pearl and Mackenzie distinguish observational, interventional, and counterfactual questions. Evidence sufficient to predict after observing a condition does not by itself identify what would happen under an intervention or what would have happened to a particular case under a contrary condition (PDF pp. 18–22, 30–49, and 236–268).

A causal target must distinguish the exposure or action, comparator, outcome, population or unit, horizon, and the kind of effect sought. Total, direct, indirect, subgroup, and case-specific effects may require different assumptions and adjustment choices (PDF pp. 132–154, 187–200, and 278–300).

Structural assumptions, identification, and estimation are distinct. More data or a more precise estimator cannot recover a causal quantity that is not identified under the proposed model and evidence (PDF pp. 125–127, 203–220, and 314–326).

Conditioning on a common effect can create an association between otherwise independent causes. Selection into a dataset, publication, survival, response, escalation, or another observed subset can therefore manufacture or reverse apparent relationships (PDF pp. 110–117, 172–200, and 310–313).

## Agent-design synthesis

Before accepting a consequential causal claim, classify the query; define the target; expose the proposed causal and observation structure; audit selection and adjustment by causal role; separate identification from estimation; and state whether the answer is identified under named assumptions, bounded, model-sensitive, observationally equivalent, or not identifiable from present evidence.

Causal diagrams make assumptions inspectable; they do not establish that those assumptions describe reality. When plausible models remain indistinguishable, identify the cheapest authorized observation, control, experiment, or reversible probe that could change the answer.

## Limits and rejected transfers

This repository does not adopt causal diagrams or do-calculus as mandatory ceremony, “control for everything,” average population effects as proof of individual causation, technical causal labels as substitutes for values or authority, or causal competence as evidence of agency, empathy, morality, consciousness, legitimacy, or authorization.

The book is a popular exposition by the principal advocate of structural causal models, not a neutral survey. Its historical judgments, 2018-era machine-learning claims, and concluding moral-AI optimism are not treated as established evidence.
